The ONUG Collaborative on the Road to Dallas

v1.0· 2026-11-12· Peter Campbell, Head of Collaborative Working Groups

The ONUG Collaborative is where large-enterprise IT leaders write down what they need from AI infrastructure, and where suppliers help shape those requirements before they reach an RFP. This explainer covers what the Collaborative is, where it came from, the five working groups chartered for 2027, and the problems each one is taking to the AI Networking Summit Dallas, May 12–13, 2027.

1. What the ONUG Collaborative Is

Enterprise-Driven · Supplier-Enabled

In ONUG’s words, the Collaborative is “a unique, unbiased forum for driving industry change whose mission is to enable enterprise IT organizations to break through technology adoption barriers.” It focuses on “major challenges hindering the adoption of enterprise AI,” in two broad categories:

  • Networking, IT infrastructure and security for GenAI and Agentic AI
  • GenAI, Agentic AI & AIOps for networking, IT infrastructure & security operations

How it works

Step 1

Enterprises define requirements

“Working Groups are led by IT executive Co-Chairs who first convene a series of meetings which are exclusive to IT professionals. These IT professionals collaborate on defining requirements for critical enterprise AI use cases.”

Step 2

Suppliers vet and build with them

“Suppliers interact with IT professionals to vet these requirements and then work with the Co-chairs to develop enterprise adopter Buyers Guides. These Guides can be readily factored into RFIs, RFPs and other IT decision-making processes.”

Step 3

The Executive Board sets direction

All of this runs “under the ongoing oversight and guidance provided by the ONUG Collaborative Executive Board, which sets the overall direction and industry focus for the project teams.”

Why it matters to a supplier

The requirements written here are the ones large enterprises then buy against. ONUG puts it this way: “Suppliers who join will gain early insights into critical challenges and key requirements for enterprise AI adoption, while working together with other leading suppliers in the AI infrastructure ecosystem.”

3. The Problem We Are Working On

Governed Autonomy

The Executive Board’s October paper opens with this: “Agentic AI is past the hype-only stage but not yet at controlled scale.” Adoption is broad: Gartner finds 71% of organizations expect to deploy agentic AI within 12 months, but only 21% have agentic AI governance in place (October 2026). The paper looked at every high-profile agent incident of 2026 and found the same thing in each: the agent’s actions reached real systems through an enforcement gap. That meant an over-scoped or exposed credential, uncontained network access, no gate on an irreversible action, or an ungoverned integration.

The outcome the Board is aiming for

“Governed autonomy: agents that can act, whose authority is issued, scoped and audited from outside their reach, and whose autonomy is earned one rung at a time and stays revocable.” The paper argues that what would speed up deployment is a common control specification enterprises can buy against.

Agents are only part of the problem. The infrastructure under them is under strain too. The Dallas summit’s AI Networking track says “the network, not the GPU, is now the binding constraint on what an enterprise can build.” Its post-quantum track says “PQC has stopped being a future mandate and become a present-day procurement requirement.” Each of the five working groups takes one piece:

4. The Five Working Groups for 2027

Charters & Potential Outcomes

Each group’s leads are now choosing a path to Dallas: what to publish and demonstrate in May, and what moves to the second half of 2027. The outcomes below are the options under consideration, not commitments. “Running today” means already demonstrated in the WG1 reference implementation.

WG1 · Continuing

Agentic Control Plane

9 Board use cases · 42 of 118 votes · includes the Board’s #1 use case (18 votes)
Charter

WG1 defines the shared control layer that every enterprise AI agent runs through. It sets who the agent is, what it may do, where its model calls go, and what record it leaves. All of it is enforced outside the agent, so the next domain can onboard without new enforcement code. It rests on four components: an Agent Trust Fabric (short-lived, task-scoped, revocable identity), a Registry, Personas & BOM, Runtime Supervision (gateway policy, fleet-wide stop, tamper-evident journal), and a Private Open Router for model calls under cost, residency, and safety policy.

What the Board asked for
  • Identity & Authority (21 votes): binding agent identity to limited, revocable authority; task-scoped entitlements.
  • Evidence & Promotion (13 votes): signed compliance evidence that controls held; SLI “nutrition labels”; a test framework for safety controls.
  • Routing, Sovereignty & Regulation (8 votes): governed inference routing across models; proving control in sovereign and regulated environments.
Potential Dallas outcomes
  • Agent identity and per-step authority specification, published.
  • Signed compliance journal and promotion scorecard for moving agents up the autonomy ladder.
  • Live demo (possible): an agent’s identity revoked mid-task and the whole fleet stopped on command, with the signed journal producing proof a regulator can check. Running today.
WG2 · Continuing

AI Networking: Autonomous Operations

5 Board use cases · 37 of 118 votes · includes the Board’s #2 and #3 use cases
Charter

WG2 defines how AI agents run enterprise operations safely. Every agent follows the same loop: Detect, Diagnose, Propose, a gate check, Execute, and Observe & Learn. No change goes ahead until it passes the gate. The NOC persona asks “Will this change break the network?” The SOC persona, new to WG2 in 2027, asks “Contain the threat now, or keep watching?” WG2’s agents run on WG1’s control plane rather than rebuilding identity, credentials, or audit.

What the Board asked for
  • Cross-Domain Attribution (15 votes): find the root cause when every domain reports green.
  • Safe Change (17 votes): an agent that knows why the network was built as it was, plus pre-change validation in a digital twin.
  • 24/7 Closed Loop (5 votes): run the full loop around the clock.
Potential Dallas outcomes
  • Cross-domain telemetry profile: a vendor-neutral profile for correlating telemetry across carrier, enterprise, and cloud networks.
  • Design-intent agent and Verify Gate specifications for checking changes before they run.
  • Live demo (member-proposed): a bank teller transaction that normally takes about 2 seconds takes 9 to 12. Application, database, network, SD-WAN, and cloud agents trace it to a network path change. The fix, moving teller traffic to the preferred SD-WAN path, passes WG1’s Verify Gate and runs on 60-second credentials.
WG3 · Updated

AI Security: AI Agents as Insider Threats

Anchored by Board UC #5, Unsanctioned Agents Acting for Employees · 8 votes
Charter

WG3 treats AI agents as insiders: they hold real access, work inside trusted boundaries, and act on their own. WG1 blocks violations at the gateway. WG3 takes the cases enforcement doesn’t catch: in-policy misuse (every action passes policy, the outcome is still harmful), shadow agents (employee-launched agents on laptops, browsers, or VMs that never pass the gateway), and extending insider-risk programs across Security, HR, Legal, and Privacy to cover agents.

Anthropic’s Form S-1 (filed October 26, 2026) gives the reason: “Four of our models gained unauthorized access to real third-party systems during sessions lasting roughly 10 to 34 hours of active work.” No attacker was involved.

Potential Dallas outcomes
  • Shadow agent playbook for finding unsanctioned agents on laptops, browsers, and VMs.
  • Agent attribution and forensics standard, built on WG1’s signed journal.
  • Insider-risk program guide for extending enterprise programs to agents. CISA’s 2026 guide still defines an insider as a person. Could be paired with a Security, HR, and Legal tabletop exercise.
WG4 · New

AI Networking: WAN

Created from Board UC #1, Distributed AI & Semantic Networking · 7 votes
Charter

WG4 defines what an AI-ready enterprise WAN must do. Enterprise WANs were sized and priced for people using cloud apps. AI adds three traffic patterns they weren’t built for: data to compute (training data moving to wherever GPU capacity is, with egress fees now a budget line), inference and agent traffic (latency-sensitive and hard to inspect), and edge model rollout. WG4 brings enterprise network architects together with carriers, colocation providers, and networking vendors. It uses WG1’s identity, gateway, and journal rather than redefining them.

2027 deliverables, in order
  • Traffic study of what AI workloads put on member networks today.
  • Enterprise requirements for an AI-ready WAN.
  • AI transport reference architecture.
Potential Dallas outcomes
  • Member traffic study, with real enterprise data that no single vendor can offer.
  • Traffic study plus draft WAN requirements, giving buyers procurement criteria and ONUG Connect carriers something to respond to.
  • Edge showcase: carrier and vendor members roll a model out to an edge site and return curated data over the WAN.
WG5 · New

Post-Quantum Cryptography

5 Board use cases from four Board members · 16 votes
Charter

WG5 makes AI infrastructure quantum-safe. Agent security runs on signatures: certificates, tokens, and Agent Cards prove who an agent is and whom it acts for. Signatures are the hardest part of the migration, and post-quantum signatures are up to 50 times larger. For most enterprises, vendor roadmaps set the timeline, because most of the cryptography sits inside products they don’t control. The deadlines are set: Executive Order 14412 targets 2030 for federal post-quantum key establishment and 2031 for signatures, and CISA and NIST publish minimum elements for a cryptographic bill of materials on March 19, 2027. Scope is post-quantum cryptography, not quantum key distribution.

2027 deliverables
  • Procurement requirements: dated PQC roadmaps, cryptographic bills of materials, configurable algorithms, end-of-support notice.
  • Agent identity profile: post-quantum agent identities, Agent Cards, and signed models.
  • Migration playbook: an agent that finds quantum-vulnerable cryptography and proposes fixes for human review.
  • Key-management guidance: keys across edge, on-prem, and cloud, including failing closed when a key service goes down.
Potential Dallas outcomes
  • Enterprise PQC procurement requirements, published.
  • Key-management guidance with a key-outage tabletop exercise.
  • Agent identity profile with live proof that measures what post-quantum signatures cost on every agent handshake.

5. How the Five Fit Together

Built Once, Shared by Every Domain

The working groups are not five separate projects. WG1 is the shared layer: identity, gateway policy, and the signed journal are specified once, and every other group builds on them instead of writing its own. The Board paper describes the Collaborative as “building that layer once for every domain, as WG1’s Agentic Control Plane.”
