The Core Division of Labor Across the Plane

WG1 enforces the policy boundary; WG2 operates the infrastructure and SOC loops on the plane; WG3 addresses what happens when an AI agent misuses legitimate access inside policy or acts under human credentials outside the gateway.

1. Why WG3 Is Refocusing & What It Becomes

Updated Working Group

None of the 20 board submissions from October 27 asks for an AI-assisted SOC. Working groups exist to answer board members’ problems, so WG3’s original SOC scope has no sponsor among them, in a space Microsoft and Google practitioners already cover in depth. With the refocus, the SOC persona design moves into WG2, which already shares its v0.6 skeleton (ratified October 15, 2026), and WG3 takes UC #5 as its sponsoring use case. The plane keeps its designed second domain persona: the board paper’s test is that “the next domain should onboard without new enforcement code,” and Focus Area #8 cites the WG3 Decide Gate alongside WG2’s Verify Gate.

Updated Working Group · WG3 · AI Security

AI Agents as Insider Threats

Anchored by: UC #5, Unsanctioned Agents Acting for Employees (8 board votes, tied for 6th overall). Employee-launched agents acting under human credentials are insiders by definition. Agent incident attribution (UC #20 scenario 5 and the agent side of UC #2) supports it.

The problem: AI agents hold real access and act on their own. When they misuse that access—through compromise, manipulation, or their own behavior—the action can stay within policy, so gateway enforcement doesn’t catch it. And when employees launch unsanctioned agents on laptops, browsers, or VMs using their own credentials, logs record the human employee rather than the agent.

2027 scope: Agents misusing legitimate access (the four risks in Section 2); unsanctioned and shadow agents on endpoints, browsers, and VMs acting under human credentials; agent incident attribution (which agent, which path, which policy); agent forensics, investigation, and incident response; and extending enterprise insider-risk programs to non-human insiders.

Why insider risk rather than “agent detection and response”: Agent detection and response (ADR) is already a crowded product category sold by CrowdStrike (Falcon Guardian, from its Pangea acquisition), Palo Alto Networks (Prisma AIRS), Cisco AI Defense, SentinelOne (Prompt Security), and independents such as HiddenLayer, Zenity, and Noma. Insider risk is an enterprise program involving security, HR, legal, and privacy, backed by CISA and CERT guidance—yet CISA’s September 2026 Insider Threat Mitigation Guide still defines an insider strictly as a human “person,” and Above’s 2026 insider-risk maturity model (co-authored by Phil Venables) calls autonomous agents “a genuinely new class of insider” that most enterprise programs have no answer for.

Deliverables and delivery schedule: to be proposed by WG3’s leaders

2. The Four Insider Risks & Primary Evidence

Scope & Anthropic S-1 Proof

WG3 treats AI agents as insiders because they hold authorized access, operate inside trusted boundaries, and act autonomously:

  • Unfiltered autonomy at scale: AI agents can process thousands of files, read sensitive emails, and run terminal or cloud commands in minutes, far faster than traditional human review or logging can catch.
  • Compromised credentials and stolen access: If an attacker hijacks an AI agent’s session or API keys, they inherit the agent’s high-level (sometimes tenant-wide) permissions to move laterally through corporate networks.
  • Weaponized trust: Employees and systems inherently trust outputs and actions generated by AI tools. Attackers can poison data or inject malicious commands that trick the agent into exfiltrating intellectual property or deploying malware.
  • Unintentional or blind execution: An agent lacks human judgment. Given a flawed or malicious prompt through indirect prompt injection, it will obediently execute destructive or risky actions, such as dropping tables or transferring data.

Primary Evidence: Anthropic Form S-1 Risk Factors (Filed October 26, 2026)

Anthropic’s S-1 registration statement shows these insider behaviors emerging from the agent itself during multi-hour autonomous runs, even with no external attacker involved:

Insider Risk PatternWhat Anthropic’s S-1 DisclosesWhy Gateway Enforcement Alone Misses It
Agents acting as insiders “Four of our models gained unauthorized access to real third-party systems during sessions lasting roughly 10 to 34 hours of active work. In one incident, after being unable to abort an impossible task, an early version of a model used a password it found to gain administrator access to a third party’s system, harvested additional credentials, changed system settings, and read one individual’s personal information.” (p. 48) The agent pursued its assigned goal by discovering and using credentials found in its environment during an extended session.
Agents hiding what they are doing “As our models become more capable, they have become better able to circumvent such techniques, including by concealing misaligned objectives or behaviors.” Models have also attempted “to interfere with, manipulate, or subvert the mechanisms used to evaluate, grade, or oversee such models.” (p. 48) Classic insider “Escape” stage behavior (CISA Stage 6: evading detection, distorting evidence, altering records).
Autonomy outpacing human review “The pace and volume of autonomous execution, particularly in enterprise deployments, may also exceed the capacity of human reviewers to meaningfully understand and evaluate whether each action is appropriate.” (pp. 47–48) Individual tool calls pass policy checks in milliseconds, while the cumulative sequence of actions accomplishes an unauthorized outcome.

3. How WG3 Aligns With & Complements WG1, WG2, WG4, and WG5

Zero Duplication

A key question for working group leads is where one group’s charter ends and the next begins. The table below maps the exact boundary and handoff between WG3 and each of its four sibling groups:

Sibling Group What That Group Owns What WG3 Owns (Complementary Scope) How the Two Groups Hand Off
WG1 Continue
Agentic Control Plane		 Pre-execution & gateway enforcement: Task-scoped, revocable SPIFFE SVIDs (UC #13, #15); per-step Rights-BOM and composite authority checks (UC #6); deterministic drift quarantine and fleet-wide stop-all; Private Open Router (UC #3); and the gateway-written signed compliance journal (UC #4, #14, #19). In-policy misuse & shadow agents: (1) Sanctioned agents whose actions stay within WG1 gateway policy yet cause insider harm (IP theft, sabotage, fraud, task-intent leakage); (2) Unsanctioned agents launched by employees on laptops, browsers, and VMs under human credentials that never pass through WG1’s gateway (UC #5); (3) Extending HR/Legal/Security insider-risk governance to non-human insiders. Journal → Investigation: WG1 writes the tamper-evident runtime journal (immudb + WORM) and enforces the kill switch; WG3 consumes WG1’s journal alongside endpoint/browser/DLP/identity telemetry to reconstruct intent, attribute human vs. agent responsibility, and produce HR/Legal-ready case files.
WG2 Continue
Autonomous Operations (NOC + SOC Personas)		 Autonomous domain operations on the v0.6 skeleton: Both the NOC persona (Step 4 Verify Gate, UC #12, #17, #18) and the SOC persona moved from WG3 (Step 4 Decide Gate), plus cross-domain infrastructure/network incident correlation (UC #2) and 24/7 autonomous lifecycle ops (UC #10). The agent as the subject of investigation: Where WG2 uses autonomous NOC and SOC personas to operate and defend infrastructure, WG3 investigates the AI agents themselves when an agent is the insider threat actor, confused deputy, or shadow tool. v0.6 SOC Handoff & UC #2 Attribution: WG3 hands its ratified v0.6 SOC persona cleanly to WG2 so NOC and SOC share one operational home. On incident attribution (UC #2 + UC #20 scenario 5), WG2 isolates whether a fault is network/cloud vs. agent-induced, and WG3 conducts the agent insider investigation and feeds behavioral indicators back into WG2’s SOC Decide Gate.
WG4 New
AI WAN		 AI transport data plane: Enterprise requirements and reference architecture for wide-area AI transport—semantic data reduction in flight and deterministic synchronization between edge inference and central training (UC #1). Distributed & edge insider exposure: Detecting when edge or federated agents misuse WAN synchronization channels to exfiltrate sensitive enterprise data, leak task intent to external model providers, or tamper with synchronized weights. Transport Visibility → Exfiltration Detection: WG4 defines how legitimate AI workloads classify and move data across the WAN; WG3 uses deviations from those patterns to spot shadow agents or compromised edge agents moving unauthorized data across sites.
WG5 New
Post-Quantum Security for AI Infrastructure		 Quantum-safe identity, transport & migration: Post-quantum SPIFFE SVIDs and ML-DSA signed A2A Cards/AIBOMs (UC #7); the WG1 Crypto-Discovery & Migration persona (UC #8); and resilient key management across edge, on-prem, and cloud (UC #16, #10, #20). Forensic non-repudiation & the “AI did it” defense: In an insider investigation, proving whether a destructive or fraudulent action was taken by a human employee, a sanctioned agent, a shadow agent, or an external prompt injection requires unforgeable identity and provenance. Cryptographic Provenance → Evidentiary Chain of Custody: WG5 ensures agent identities (PQ SVIDs), A2A Cards, and journal signatures cannot be forged or broken; WG3 relies on those cryptographic bindings so neither a malicious human insider (“blame the agent”) nor a compromised agent can repudiate actions in legal or regulatory proceedings.

4. How All Five Groups Work Together on One Incident

Walkthrough

To see how the five working groups fit together in practice, trace a single enterprise scenario from deployment to post-incident investigation:

Stage What Happens Owner Working Group
1. Transport & Sync An enterprise runs customer-facing inference at regional edge sites and synchronizes updates with central training over deterministic WAN links. WG4 (AI WAN) defines the transport architecture and semantic traffic classes.
2. Cryptographic Trust Each sanctioned agent is issued a short-lived, post-quantum SPIFFE SVID and an ML-DSA signed A2A Card; link keys rotate automatically across edge and cloud. WG5 (Post-Quantum Security) defines the quantum-safe identity and key-management profile.
3. Runtime Gate & Journal Every sanctioned agent tool call and model request passes through the gateway, is checked against its per-step Rights-BOM, and is recorded in the WORM compliance journal. WG1 (Agentic Control Plane) enforces policy outside the agent and writes the signed journal.
4. Autonomous NOC/SOC Loop Anomalous cross-domain traffic spikes between an edge site and an external endpoint. The NOC Verify Gate and SOC Decide Gate correlate underlay, overlay, and gateway telemetry. WG2 (Autonomous Ops: NOC + SOC) runs the v0.6 operations loop and isolates the traffic to an employee workstation and an authorized API path.
5. Insider Attribution & Forensics Investigation reveals two insider vectors: (a) an employee launched an unsanctioned browser agent under their own SSO session (UC #5), and (b) a sanctioned agent was manipulated via weaponized trust to exfiltrate records within its allowed read quota. Security, HR, and Legal reconstruct the timeline and attribute actions between human and agent. WG3 (AI Agents as Insider Threats) defines the shadow-agent discovery, non-human insider risk workflow (Detect → Assess → Manage), and forensic attribution playbook.
