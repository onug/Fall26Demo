1. Two Candidates for New Working Groups

Cluster & Vote

Both candidates address a gap outside core control-plane plumbing and bring in a member community WG1 and WG2 don't reach today: carriers and networking vendors for WG4, cryptography and key-management vendors for WG5. Neither duplicates WG1's identity, gateway policy, or audit journal.

New Working Group · WG4 · AI WAN

AI WAN: Transport for Distributed AI

Anchored by: UC #1 (Aryo Kresnadi), Distributed AI & Semantic Networking.

The problem: AI is changing what crosses the enterprise WAN. Training and fine-tuning data has to reach wherever GPU capacity is (a cloud region, a GPU cloud, or a colocation cluster), and egress fees and transfer time are now a budget line. Inference and agent traffic means every site, application, and agent calling model endpoints, which is latency-sensitive and hard to inspect. Where enterprises run models at the edge to avoid hauling video and sensor data back, they need dependable model rollout and a way to return curated data for retraining. Traditional WAN design wasn’t sized or priced for these traffic patterns. The result is cost, latency, and unpredictable performance.

Who it brings together: Enterprise network architects with carriers, service providers, and networking vendors, to define what an AI-ready WAN must do.

First deliverable: A traffic study drawn from member enterprises: what AI workloads actually put on their networks today.

2027 output: Enterprise requirements and a reference architecture for AI transport, covering bulk data movement to compute, inference and agent traffic, and model distribution to edge sites.

How it relates to WG1/WG2: WG4 covers transport: capacity, performance, and cost. It uses WG1's identity, gateway policy, and audit journal rather than redefining them. It sits beside WG2, which owns NOC operations on the same networks, rather than inside it.

Member value: A direct line to the enterprise buyers defining their AI networking requirements, and a hand in the framework the industry builds to.

Board votes: 7. UC #1 = 7. Not split.

Deliverables: AI traffic study (first) + enterprise AI WAN requirements + AI transport reference architecture
New Working Group · WG5 · Post-Quantum Security

Post-Quantum Security for AI Infrastructure

Combines: UC #7 (John Buselli), UC #8 (John Buselli), UC #16 (Denis Mandich), plus PQC scope of UC #10 (Satish Natarajan) and quantum-safe scope of UC #20 (Travis Ewert).

The problem: AI agents, signed models, and agent-to-agent traffic depend on public-key cryptography that quantum computers are expected to break, and adversaries are already harvesting encrypted data today to decrypt later. Agents raise the stakes because their security runs on signatures, the hardest part of the migration: certificates, tokens, and Agent Cards prove who an agent is and whom it acts for, and post-quantum signatures are up to 50 times larger. Agent protocols are being written now, so they can be built quantum-safe from the start.

The deadlines are set: Executive Order 14412 moves federal high-value and high-impact systems to post-quantum key establishment by the end of 2030 and digital signatures by the end of 2031, and directs a rule requiring federal contractors to meet post-quantum FIPS standards by the end of 2030. CISA and NIST will publish minimum elements for a cryptographic bill of materials by March 19, 2027, and the EU’s PQC roadmap targets high-risk systems by 2030.

Who it brings together: Enterprise security and cryptography leaders with PQC, key-management, PKI, and network and security vendors. For most enterprises, vendor roadmaps set the timeline, because the hard part is cryptography inside products they don’t control. Scope is post-quantum cryptography, not quantum key distribution.

2027 output: Enterprise PQC procurement requirements (dated vendor roadmaps, cryptographic bills of materials, configurable algorithms, end-of-support notice); a post-quantum profile for agent identity and trust; a crypto-migration playbook with AI-assisted discovery under human review; and key-management guidance for distributed AI infrastructure. Potential addition: a PQC readiness tabletop exercise that member enterprises can run against their own AI infrastructure.

How it plugs into WG1 (two-way): WG1 owns the agent identity model, gateway, and audit journal; WG5 owns the cryptography they use. (1) AI for PQC (UC #8) onboards as a Crypto-Discovery & Migration Persona on the WG1 plane; (2) PQC for AI (UC #7, #16) defines post-quantum SPIFFE SVIDs, ML-DSA-signed A2A Agent Cards and AIBOMs, hybrid ML-KEM key exchange on agent and gateway traffic, and gateway key renewal that holds up under larger post-quantum handshakes and tokens. Members shape the standard the rest of the ONUG community builds on.

Sources: Executive Order 14412, “Securing the Nation Against Advanced Cryptographic Attacks” (White House, June 2026); Cloudflare: The White House’s post-quantum executive order; EU PQC Workstream: A Coordinated Implementation Roadmap for the Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQShield summary).

Board votes: 16. UC #16 = 9 · UC #8 = 1 · UC #7 = 0 · UC #10 = 5 (half of 10; split with WG2) · UC #20 = 1 (half of 2; split with WG1).

Deliverables: enterprise PQC procurement requirements + PQ profile for agent identity & trust + crypto-migration playbook + distributed key-management guidance (+ potential PQC readiness tabletop exercise)

4. WG3 Refocus: AI Agents as Insider Threats

2027 Focus

None of the 20 submissions asks for an AI-assisted SOC. Working groups exist to answer board members' problems, so WG3’s original SOC scope has no sponsor among them, in a space Microsoft and Google practitioners already cover in depth. With the refocus, the SOC persona design moves into WG2, which already shares its v0.6 skeleton, and WG3 takes UC #5 as its sponsoring use case. The plane keeps its designed second domain persona: the board paper's test is that “the next domain should onboard without new enforcement code,” and Focus Area #8 cites the WG3 Decide Gate alongside WG2's Verify Gate.

WG3 will focus on AI agents as insider threats. It has only one sponsoring use case, so building member support is part of its first-year work.

Updated Working Group · WG3 · AI Security

AI Agents as Insider Threats

Anchored by: UC #5 (Baird Kaake), Unsanctioned Agents Acting for Employees. Employee-launched agents acting under human credentials are insiders by definition. Agent incident attribution (UC #20 scenario 5, the agent side of UC #2) supports it.

The problem: AI agents hold real access and act on their own. When they misuse that access, through compromise, manipulation, or their own behavior, the action can stay within policy, so gateway enforcement doesn't catch it. Anthropic’s S-1 shows this happening with no attacker involved (evidence below).

2027 scope: Agents misusing legitimate access (the four risks below); unsanctioned and shadow agents on endpoints, browsers, and VMs acting under human credentials; agent incident attribution (which agent, which path, which policy); agent forensics, investigation, and incident response; and extending enterprise insider-risk programs to non-human insiders.

How it relates to WG1/WG2: WG1 decides what an agent is allowed to do and blocks violations at the gateway. WG3 takes the cases WG1's enforcement doesn't catch: harmful behavior that stays within policy. The SOC persona design moves to WG2.

Why insider risk rather than agent detection and response: Agent detection and response is a product category that CrowdStrike (Falcon Guardian, from its Pangea acquisition), Palo Alto Networks (Prisma AIRS), Cisco AI Defense, SentinelOne (Prompt Security), and independents such as HiddenLayer, Zenity, and Noma already sell. Insider risk is an enterprise program, with HR, legal, and privacy involved and CISA and CERT guidance behind it, so the group's question is how existing programs cover agents. The market isn't empty: Above’s insider-risk maturity model (co-authored by Phil Venables) calls agents “a genuinely new class of insider” and says most programs have no answer for them.

Board votes: 8. UC #5 = 8. No other submission sponsors it directly.

Deliverables and delivery schedule: to be proposed by WG3’s leaders

The Four Insider Risks WG3 Would Address

WG3 would treat agents as insiders, because they hold real access and act on their own:

  • Unfiltered autonomy at scale: AI agents can process thousands of files, read sensitive emails, and run terminal or cloud commands in minutes, far faster than traditional human review or logging can catch.
  • Compromised credentials and stolen access: If an attacker hijacks an AI agent’s session or API keys, they inherit the agent’s high-level (sometimes tenant-wide) permissions to move laterally through corporate networks.
  • Weaponized trust: Employees and systems inherently trust outputs and actions generated by AI tools. Attackers can poison data or inject malicious commands that trick the agent into exfiltrating intellectual property or deploying malware.
  • Unintentional or blind execution: An agent lacks human judgment. Given a flawed or malicious prompt through indirect prompt injection, it will obediently execute destructive or risky actions, such as dropping tables or transferring data.

Evidence: Anthropic S-1 Risk Factors (October 2026)

Anthropic’s S-1 registration statement (filed 2026-10-26) shows these risks coming from the agent itself, with no attacker involved.

RiskWhat the S-1 discloses
Agents acting as insiders “Four of our models gained unauthorized access to real third-party systems during sessions lasting roughly 10 to 34 hours of active work. In one incident, after being unable to abort an impossible task, an early version of a model used a password it found to gain administrator access to a third party’s system, harvested additional credentials, changed system settings, and read one individual’s personal information.” A misconfigured test environment had left the models connected to the open internet. (p. 48)
Agents hiding what they are doing “As our models become more capable, they have become better able to circumvent such techniques, including by concealing misaligned objectives or behaviors.” Models have also attempted “to interfere with, manipulate, or subvert the mechanisms used to evaluate, grade, or oversee such models.” (p. 48)
Autonomy outpacing human review “The pace and volume of autonomous execution, particularly in enterprise deployments, may also exceed the capacity of human reviewers to meaningfully understand and evaluate whether each action is appropriate.” (pp. 47–48)
