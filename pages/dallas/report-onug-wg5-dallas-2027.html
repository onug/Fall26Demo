WG5 for 2027: Post-Quantum Security for AI Infrastructure on the Road to Dallas

For review with Prospective WG5 Leads· Backed by 5 Board Use Cases (16 Votes)· Peter Campbell, Head of Collaborative Working Groups

AI agents, signed models, and agent-to-agent traffic depend on public-key cryptography that quantum computers are expected to break, and adversaries are already harvesting encrypted data today to decrypt later. Agents raise the stakes because their security runs on signatures, the hardest part of the migration: certificates, tokens, and Agent Cards prove who an agent is and whom it acts for, and post-quantum signatures are up to 50 times larger. Agent protocols are being written now, so they can be built quantum-safe from the start.

The deadlines are set. Executive Order 14412 moves federal high-value and high-impact systems to post-quantum key establishment by the end of 2030 and digital signatures by the end of 2031, and directs a rule requiring federal contractors to meet post-quantum FIPS standards by the end of 2030. CISA and NIST will publish minimum elements for a cryptographic bill of materials by March 19, 2027, and the EU’s PQC roadmap targets high-risk systems by 2030.

For most enterprises, vendor roadmaps set the timeline, because the hard part is cryptography inside products they don’t control. WG5 brings enterprise security and cryptography leaders together with PQC, key-management, PKI, and network and security vendors; its scope is post-quantum cryptography, not quantum key distribution. In 2027 it will produce enterprise PQC procurement requirements (dated vendor roadmaps, cryptographic bills of materials, configurable algorithms, end-of-support notice), a post-quantum profile for agent identity and trust, a crypto-migration playbook with AI-assisted discovery under human review, and key-management guidance for distributed AI infrastructure; a PQC readiness tabletop exercise is a potential addition. Its work plugs directly into ONUG’s Agentic Control Plane, so members shape the standard the rest of the ONUG community builds on.

Why PQC + AI Infrastructure Is a Dedicated Working Group

Four independent board members across financial services, defense, telecom, and data center operations submitted five cryptographic and post-quantum proposals at the October 27 board meeting. Combining them into WG5 unites two sides of the same coin: using governed AI agents, under human review, to discover and migrate vulnerable enterprise cryptography (AI for PQC) and moving the signatures agent security runs on (certificates, tokens, Agent Cards, AIBOMs, and signed models) and distributed key management to post-quantum primitives while agent protocols are still being written (PQC for AI). Because vendor roadmaps set most enterprises’ timelines, WG5 also writes what enterprises should require of those vendors.

1. WG5’s 2027 Deliverables & The Regulatory Clock

New Working Group

With CISA’s CBOM minimum elements due March 19, 2027—just eight weeks before the AI Networking Summit Dallas (May 12–13, 2027)—and Executive Order 14412 setting 2030 and 2031 deadlines for federal high-value and high-impact systems and directing a 2030 rule for federal contractors, PQC has shifted from a research topic to an active procurement and architecture requirement. For most enterprises the binding constraint is their vendors’ roadmaps, so WG5 structures its 2027 work around four deliverables, starting with procurement requirements (plus an optional tabletop exercise):

Deliverable 1 · Procurement (All 5 Use Cases)

Enterprise PQC Procurement Requirements

Putting the vendor roadmap on the enterprise’s timeline:

Most of the cryptography an enterprise must migrate sits inside products it doesn’t control. WG5 specifies what members should require of their vendors: dated PQC roadmaps, cryptographic bills of materials, configurable algorithms (crypto agility), and end-of-support notice for quantum-vulnerable cryptography.

Answers the Dallas PQC track’s framing of PQC as a present-day procurement requirement
Deliverable 2 · PQC for AI (UC #7, #20)

Post-Quantum Profile for Agent Identity & Trust

Upgrading WG1’s Agent Trust Fabric to NIST FIPS 203/204/205:

Specifies post-quantum SPIFFE SVIDs (ML-DSA signatures), ML-DSA-signed A2A Agent Cards and AIBOMs, signed-model provenance, hybrid ML-KEM key exchange on agent and gateway traffic, and gateway key renewal that holds up under larger post-quantum handshakes and tokens. WG1 keeps the identity model; WG5 defines the cryptography it uses.

Plugs directly into WG1 Component #1 (Agent Trust Fabric) & Component #2 (Registry/BOM)
Deliverable 3 · AI for PQC (UC #8, #10)

Agentic Crypto-Discovery & Migration Playbook

AI-assisted discovery under human review:

Defines the Crypto-Discovery & Migration Persona that onboards onto WG1’s Agentic Control Plane to inventory hardcoded certificates, TLS libraries, SSH keys, and API tokens across repos, containers, and network appliances, generate a CISA-aligned CBOM, and propose prioritized remediation changes for human review.

Onboards onto WG1 Control Plane + validates via WG2 Digital Twin
Deliverable 4 · Distributed Key Resiliency (UC #16)

Resilient Key Management Across Distributed AI Infra

Keeping distributed AI safe across edge, on-prem, and multi-cloud:

Provides architecture and operational guidance for cross-gateway key residency, automated rotation of short-lived agent credentials, and fail-closed recovery when a regional KMS, HSM, or identity provider becomes unreachable—plus a potential PQC Readiness Tabletop Exercise.

Anchored by UC #16 (Denis Mandich · 9 votes, Rank #5 overall)

4. How WG5 Aligns With & Complements WG1, WG2, WG3, and WG4

Cross-Group Architecture

Rather than operating as an isolated cryptography island, WG5 plugs directly into ONUG’s Agentic Control Plane and coordinates with every sibling working group:

Sibling Group How WG5 & That Group Complement Each Other Concrete Technical Handoff
WG1 Continue
Agentic Control Plane		 Two-Way Plug-In (AI for PQC + PQC for AI): WG1 owns the agent identity model, gateway, and audit journal; WG5 owns the cryptography they use. WG5 does not build a separate control plane; it both runs a persona on WG1 and upgrades WG1’s cryptographic primitives. 1. AI for PQC (UC #8): WG5’s Crypto-Discovery & Migration Agent onboards as a governed domain persona on WG1’s plane.
2. PQC for AI (UC #7, #16, #20): WG5 defines the post-quantum SPIFFE SVIDs (ML-DSA), hybrid ML-KEM key exchange on agent and gateway traffic, and OpenBao key-resiliency patterns that WG1’s Agent Trust Fabric adopts.
WG2 Continue
Autonomous Operations (NOC + SOC Personas)		 Joint Split of UC #10 (Satish Natarajan, 10 votes): Rolling out post-quantum certificates and larger ML-DSA/ML-KEM keys across production networks risks MTU packet fragmentation, handshake timeouts, and KMS throttling. WG2’s Digital Twin & Pre-Change Verify Gate (UC #12/#18) serves as the pre-deployment proving ground to simulate WG5’s PQC migrations and KMS fail-closed recovery scenarios before Step 5 production execution.
WG3 Updated
AI Security: AI Agents as Insider Threats		 Cryptographic Non-Repudiation for Insider Forensics: WG3 investigates sanctioned and shadow AI agents misusing access (UC #5). WG5’s post-quantum agent identity profile and quantum-safe Merkle signatures in WG1’s compliance journal ensure that agent actions, delegating human principals, and tool-call evidence remain non-repudiable against future cryptographic forgery.
WG4 New
AI WAN		 Quantum-Safe Protection for AI Traffic in Transit: Training and fine-tuning data moving to GPU capacity, inference and agent traffic to model endpoints, and models distributed to edge sites (WG4) are targets for “harvest now, decrypt later” collection. WG5 defines quantum-safe transport profiles (hybrid ML-KEM TLS/IPsec/MACsec and cross-site key residency, UC #16/#20) that protect WG4’s data-to-compute, inference, and edge model-distribution links without breaking WAN latency budgets. WG4 covers capacity, performance, and cost; WG5 covers the cryptography.

5. Input Needed from Prospective WG5 Leads for the Road to Dallas

May 12–13, 2027 Target

At the AI Networking Summit Dallas (May 12–13, 2027, Hyatt Regency Frisco), Post-Quantum Cryptography has its own dedicated track—framed around “PQC has stopped being a future mandate and become a present-day procurement requirement,” just after CISA and NIST publish CBOM minimum elements (due March 19, 2027). To launch WG5 and set its 2027 schedule, we are collecting input from prospective WG5 leads on five questions:

Decision Area Context & Options Question for Prospective WG5 Leads
1. Sequencing the Four 2027 Deliverables Around March 19 & May 12, 2027 CISA’s CBOM minimum elements land March 19, 2027, and the Dallas Summit follows on May 12–13, 2027. WG5’s four deliverables are: (1) Enterprise PQC Procurement Requirements, (2) Post-Quantum Profile for Agent Identity & Trust, (3) Crypto-Migration Playbook (AI-assisted CBOM discovery under human review), and (4) Distributed Key-Management Guidance. Which deliverable should WG5 prioritize for v0.1 release at the May 2027 Dallas Summit (e.g., the Procurement Requirements, which match the Dallas track’s procurement framing, plus the PQ Agent Identity Profile), and which should target H2 2027?
2. Designing the PQC Readiness Tabletop Exercise for Dallas The triage report notes a PQC Readiness Tabletop Exercise as a potential high-impact addition that member enterprises can run against their own AI infrastructure. Should WG5 run a live PQC & Distributed Key Outage Tabletop Exercise with enterprise security leaders at the Dallas Summit in May 2027?
3. Specifying the onug-plane PQC Proof (WG5 ↔ WG1/WG2) In onug-plane, agents currently use standard X.509/JWT SPIRE SVIDs and OpenBao credentials. Adding a hybrid/ML-DSA SVID profile or onboarding a Rung 1 pqc-observer (CBOM discovery agent) would give WG5 a live demonstration at Dallas. Which technical proof should we build first in onug-plane for Dallas: (A) Hybrid/ML-DSA SPIRE SVIDs + ML-KEM mTLS benchmark on agent identity and gateway traffic, or (B) a Rung 1 CBOM Crypto-Discovery Agent Persona?
4. Engaging the 4 Board Submitters as Founding Authors Four board members sponsored WG5’s five use cases: Denis Mandich (UC #16, 9v), Satish Natarajan (UC #10, 10v split), John Buselli (UC #7 & #8), and Travis Ewert (UC #20, 2v split). How should we structure drafting sub-teams across these four enterprise board sponsors and incoming PQC/PKI/KMS vendor participants?
5. Quantifying PQC Performance Impact on Agent Fleets Agent security runs on signatures (certificates, tokens, and Agent Cards), and post-quantum signatures are up to 50 times larger, so ML-DSA signature size and ML-KEM key establishment overhead land on every agent handshake and token. What benchmark metrics (handshake latency, packet fragmentation over WAN/overlay links, KMS signing throughput, fail-closed recovery time) should WG5 measure and publish in its 2027 guidance?
