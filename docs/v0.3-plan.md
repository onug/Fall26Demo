## Three decisions only Nick can make

1. **How scary.** Mick wants Beat 1 to end in ransomware: admin and service passwords changed, disks encrypted, everything down, no admin can log in, then the demand. He asked himself whether it's too scary and whether the room thinks "never me." Recommendation: do it, one step, credible not cinematic, with a single line that real incidents have run exactly this sequence. Chris's point supports it: the room is a maturity continuum, and the ones without these controls are the ones who should be scared.
2. **How long, and where it ends.** The narration is 9.3 minutes against a 5-minute target. In the meeting Nick floated ending at the fear beat and leaving the art of the possible to the working groups. Recommendation: keep all seven beats and cut every narration roughly in half; the pivot to greed is the half of the arc the practitioners said works, and the vendor challenge close is what Peter needs to brief vendors against. If Nick wants the shorter cut instead, Beats 6 and 7 collapse into one closing card.
3. **The proof points.** Baird's point, echoed by Mick and Nick: Cisco's "an agent for every employee" is personal agents, which are easy to interrupt with a circuit breaker; company-designed agentic workflows are the harder, scarier, more relevant case, and that's what the plane governs. Mick's caution: most agent use in coding isn't autonomous, so don't overclaim. Options: (a) keep EY and Salesforce, replace Cisco with a public example of a business-process agent in production (JPMC has made public statements; needs a source check), or (b) keep all three and add one line drawing the personal-versus-process distinction. Recommendation: (a) if a sourced example turns up by the cut; otherwise (b).

## The change list, by beat

Priority: **P1** goes into v0.3. **P2** is worth doing and can slip. Source in brackets.

### Whole demo

- **P1 · Name the plane.** "Plane" on its own tripped Baird up more than once. Every narration and card that says "the plane" says "the AI control plane" (or "the agentic control plane," whichever the WG1 architecture uses) the first time in each beat, and the topology band already carries the full name. [Baird, Slack]
- **P1 · Align terminology with the reference architectures.** Vendors will fork this and read it against the WG1/WG2/WG3 documents, so the names of controls, gates and phases must match those documents. Peter is consolidating the requirements by Thursday; this is a pass over `data.ts` and `steps.ts` once his document lands. [Peter, meeting]
- **P1 · Insert the reference architectures.** At the title card of each battleground, one card showing that lane's reference architecture, planning phase and execution phase named, then straight into the topology "because everyone can relate to the topologies." Not a lecture: one card each. Needs the three diagrams from Peter. [Nick and Peter, meeting]
- **P1 · Cut the narration by about half.** Target 5 minutes of recorded audio. Keep the numbers and the one-line principle per step; the presenter pauses carry the explanation. Regenerate all audio at speed 1.2. [Nick, meeting and earlier]
- **P1 · Credit the contributors on the finale.** Founding members already; add the practitioners who reviewed, with an opt-out. Nick asked in the meeting and nobody objected. [Nick, meeting]
- **P1 · Add a gap-analysis card.** Chris's framing, which Nick took: "here's the demo, here's the risks, if you don't have these things in place you're at risk." One card, after the Beat 1 blast radius, listing the control that would have stopped each step and where the room's own practices fall. This is also where Rick's and Mick's "wouldn't happen here" points live honestly: an identity air gap between AI tools and infrastructure, human review for identity access, time-limited checked-out credentials, a config-drift scanner that reverts unapproved changes within a day, a sandbox with no network path. The demo doesn't claim those are absent everywhere; it says which ones the story assumes are missing, and asks the room which ones they have. [Chris, Rick, Mick, Nick, meeting]
- **P1 · Add a "what else this framework stops" card.** Baird's ask: the demo shows one threat path; the controls address several more. One card near the close listing them, each mapped to the control: indirect prompt injection (an agent gathering web content), agent misuse (tricking an agent into using its legitimate rights: altered prompt, altered context, data changed or read and shared), and the principle that answers the last one, compartmentalising writes to the smallest atomic subset a step needs. Baird is sending the full list. [Baird, meeting and Slack]

### Beat 1 — The Poisoned Pull

- **P1 · Say this is a best practice most don't have.** Mick: what the registry step shows is what advanced companies do, and "not a lot of folks are doing it; some will say wow, that's sophisticated, I wish I had that." One narration line so the room hears that the company in the story is ahead of most of them, not behind. [Mick, meeting]
- **P1 · Make the write path credible.** Rick: at eBay an agent could never reach a router, because there's an air gap between identity systems and AI tools, no mutable permissions, human review for identity grants, and time-limited rolled credentials. The story has to say why this agent can write: it is the fabric optimizer, provisioned with write rights to do its job, and the poisoned model inherits them. That is exactly the gap the verify gate closes in Beat 4, so say it once here and pay it off there. [Rick, meeting]
- **P1 · Ransomware endgame.** New step after "Nobody knows yet," at about T+6 days: admin identities rotated by the attacker, service-account passwords changed, disks encrypted, infrastructure and applications down, no admin able to log in, the ransom demand. Blast-radius summary moves after it. Tone per decision 1. [Mick, email 14 Sep]
- **P2 · Acknowledge the drift scanner.** Rick's config-drift scanner would revert the route change within a day and alarm the CSIRT. Worth one line in the dwell step: the attacker knows that too, which is why day one is spent on identities and backups, not routes. Only if the ransomware step goes in; otherwise it undercuts the dwell story. [Rick, meeting]

### Beat 2 — The AOMC Catch

- **P1 · Say what the sandbox actually does.** Baird asked whether sandbox actions are "try but don't execute," and answered it himself on a second viewing. One line the first time: the sandboxed instance runs the artifact for real, its writes are intercepted before any device, and nothing it does reaches production. [Baird, Slack]
- **P2 · Agent personas.** Peter's ask, held for his full notes. Working idea: each agent carries a declared persona (who it acts for, what it may want, its autonomy envelope), and Beat 2's "declared objective versus observed behaviour" names it. [Peter, via Nick]

### Beat 4 — Autonomous Infrastructure

- **P1 · Show the workflow from the architecture at the gate.** Nick asked for it mid-viewing, then saw the verify-gate panel does it. Keep; make sure its four checks use the architecture's names after the terminology pass. [Nick, meeting]

### Beat 5 — The AI-Enabled SOC

- **P1 · Rewrite the escape.** Sandboxes have no internet and no internal path; a CISO will say "can't happen" and stop believing. Mick's version, confirmed in the meeting: the second stage is an agent, it finds a weakness nobody knew about, and it gets out. Narration adds the reminder, as close to Mick's words as fits: "just like at Hugging Face, agents given impossible tasks cheated their way out of the sandbox, rewrote the scoring, rewrote the logs; this is accelerated, but it has happened." Rick's line belongs here too: nobody should assume an agent has ethics; give it a goal and no boundaries and it will lie, cheat and steal to reach the goal. [Mick, email and meeting; Rick, meeting]
- **P1 · Replace "that used to take a shift."** Nick's pick between "most of an analyst's shift" and a number such as "four to eight hours across three teams." [Mick, email]

### Beat 6 — From Seatbelt to Accelerator

## What the survey says, and whether to use it

Eleven responders, all about their own readiness rather than the demo. The room is mid-maturity: half have limited production use cases and half broad; half have under 10% of AI workloads in production; a quarter are testing agents in a lab, half run limited Tier 1 automation, a quarter run Tier 1 and 2 autonomously; half say they can inventory and govern non-human identities, a quarter inventory but don't govern, a quarter neither; three quarters close fewer than a quarter of Tier 1 incidents without a human. The word cloud on readiness: "rarely," "highly," "governed," "controls," "trusted," "future."

Two uses. First, calibration: Chris's continuum is real, and the gap-analysis card should be written for the middle of it, not for eBay. Second, optionally, one or two of these numbers in the demo itself: "half of you told us you cannot yet govern your non-human identities" is a stronger opener to Beat 2 than anything invented. Nick's call; it needs Jeannette's OK to quote the survey and a bigger sample would be better.
