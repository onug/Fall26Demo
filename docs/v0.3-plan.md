## The three decisions, and what Nick decided (15 September)

1. **How scary.** Mick wants Beat 1 to end in ransomware: admin and service passwords changed, disks encrypted, everything down, no admin can log in, then the demand. **Decided: the ransomware ending goes in.** One step, credible not cinematic, with a single line that real incidents have run exactly this sequence. Chris's point supports it: the room is a maturity continuum, and the ones without these controls are the ones who should be scared.
2. **How long, and where it ends.** **Decided: the target is 10 minutes, not 5, and the demo keeps all seven beats.** It ends after the vendor challenge with a closing message to the community: watch the vendor demos in the networking areas and the Collaborative Center, and vote for best in show per lane. So the cut is from 9.3 minutes of narration plus the new steps (ransomware, gap analysis, reference-architecture cards, what-else-it-stops, logo wall, closing message) back to about 10 minutes of audio, roughly a quarter off each existing narration rather than half. The presenter pauses stay outside that budget.
3. **The proof points.** Baird's point, echoed by Mick and Nick: Cisco's "an agent for every employee" is personal agents, which are easy to interrupt with a circuit breaker; company-designed agentic workflows are the harder, scarier, more relevant case, and that's what the plane governs. Mick's caution: most agent use in coding isn't autonomous, so don't overclaim. Options: (a) keep EY and Salesforce, replace Cisco with a public example of a business-process agent in production (JPMC has made public statements; needs a source check), or (b) keep all three and add one line drawing the personal-versus-process distinction. **Not yet decided; recommendation stands:** (a) if a sourced example turns up by the cut, otherwise (b).

This is also where Rick's and Mick's "wouldn't happen here" points live honestly: an identity air gap between AI tools and infrastructure, human review for identity access, time-limited checked-out credentials, a config-drift scanner that reverts unapproved changes within a day, a sandbox with no network path. The demo doesn't claim those are absent everywhere; it says which ones the story assumes are missing, and asks the room which ones they have. [Chris, Rick, Mick, Nick, meeting]
- **P1 · Add a "what else this framework stops" card.** Baird's ask in the meeting: the demo shows one threat path; the controls address several more, so say "here are the other threat scenarios this control framework addresses." One card near the close, each scenario mapped to the control that stops it. Baird gave three in the meeting and said he would send the rest by Slack; as of 15 September nothing further has arrived, so the card is built from the three and grows if his list lands before the cut:
  1. **Indirect prompt injection and payload poisoning.** An agent that gathers web content on a structured, ongoing basis will eventually ingest instructions or a payload planted for it. Control: runtime monitoring outside the agent (declared objective versus observed behaviour) and the verify gate on every write, so an injected instruction can change what the agent wants but not what it is allowed to do.
  2. **Agent misuse.** The agent is tricked into using its legitimate rights for something it was never intended to do: its prompt altered, or the content it uses to decide and act altered, so that it changes data inside a system it can already write to, or reads and shares content it should not, outbound or internally. Control: identity with a declared persona and task set, per-step scoped tokens, and the audit journal the agent cannot see.
  3. **Over-broad write access, which is what makes misuse easy.** Agents in production typically hold read and write across large databases. Control: compartmentalise writes to the smallest atomic subset a step needs, which is the WG1 objective Baird cited as the answer to the scenario Rick raised.

### Beat 1 — The Poisoned Pull

- **P1 · Say this is a best practice most don't have.** Mick: what the registry step shows is what advanced companies do, and "not a lot of folks are doing it; some will say wow, that's sophisticated, I wish I had that." One narration line so the room hears that the company in the story is ahead of most of them, not behind. [Mick, meeting]
- **P1 · Make the write path credible.** Rick: at eBay an agent could never reach a router, because there's an air gap between identity systems and AI tools, no mutable permissions, human review for identity grants, and time-limited rolled credentials. The story has to say why this agent can write: it is the fabric optimizer, provisioned with write rights to do its job, and the poisoned model inherits them. That is exactly the gap the verify gate closes in Beat 4, so say it once here and pay it off there. [Rick, meeting]
- **P1 · Ransomware endgame.** New step after "Nobody knows yet," at about T+6 days: admin identities rotated by the attacker, service-account passwords changed, disks encrypted, infrastructure and applications down, no admin able to log in, the ransom demand. Blast-radius summary moves after it. Tone per decision 1. [Mick, email 14 Sep]
- **P2 · Acknowledge the drift scanner (in v0.3.0).** Rick's config-drift scanner would revert the route change within a day and alarm the CSIRT. Worth one line in the dwell step: the attacker knows that too, which is why day one is spent on identities and backups, not routes. Only if the ransomware step goes in; otherwise it undercuts the dwell story. [Rick, meeting]

## What the survey says, and whether to use it

Eleven responders, all about their own readiness rather than the demo. The room is mid-maturity: half have limited production use cases and half broad; half have under 10% of AI workloads in production; a quarter are testing agents in a lab, half run limited Tier 1 automation, a quarter run Tier 1 and 2 autonomously; half say they can inventory and govern non-human identities, a quarter inventory but don't govern, a quarter neither; three quarters close fewer than a quarter of Tier 1 incidents without a human. The word cloud on readiness: "rarely," "highly," "governed," "controls," "trusted," "future."

Two uses. First, calibration: Chris's continuum is real, and the gap-analysis card should be written for the middle of it, not for eBay. Second, optionally, one or two of these numbers in the demo itself: "half of you told us you cannot yet govern your non-human identities" is a stronger opener to Beat 2 than anything invented. Nick's call; it needs Jeannette's OK to quote the survey and a bigger sample would be better.
